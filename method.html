Method

One photo. One voice. A mind that remembers. A real-time AI actor that talks with you in its own voice, shows emotion in its face, takes spoken direction like an actor, and plays scenes with a second actor. It runs entirely on one home PC.

At a glance

What it is
A real-time, directable AI actor: a photoreal head, a body, a voice and a personality.
What goes in
One face photo, a 5 to 15 second voice clip, and a line about who the character is. The AI writes the personality from that line, and every line of it can be edited by hand.
What comes out
A character you talk to, direct, put in two-person scenes, and view in VR.
Runs on
One Windows PC (RTX 4090 and RTX 5060 Ti), viewed in Chrome, on a Meta Quest 3, or on a phone over home Wi-Fi. No cloud, no paid services.
Built with
three.js and WebXR (TypeScript), a Python FastAPI server, and local AI models: Qwen 27B through Ollama, faster-whisper, Chatterbox Turbo and IndexTTS voice cloning, LAM one-photo Gaussian heads, and the FLAME face model.
Status
Personal proof of concept, under active development. Not for sale or distribution.

From a photo to a performance

No hand-built 3D assets anywhere in between.

  1. Make a character

    Drop in a photo and give it a name. The server builds a photoreal Gaussian splat head, rigs it onto an animated face, picks skin and eye colours, keeps or grows the hair, and writes the character file.

  2. Give it a life

    The local language model drafts a long-form personality from one line or a pasted bio: a short core, sections like History and Wants and fears, and memories in the character's own words.

  3. Give it a voice

    Any short reference clip becomes the character's voice. Two speech engines, switchable live.

  4. Talk to it

    Speak or type. It answers in its own voice within a few seconds, with lip sync, blinks, glances, breathing, and an emotion that shows in its face, head and voice.

  5. Direct it

    Mix plain notes into the conversation: "look angry", "a bit more skeptical", "look left", "sit". It obeys silently, the way an actor takes a note, until released.

  6. Stage a scene

    Two characters side by side, improvised or half-scripted. They look at each other as they speak while staying open to the camera, and the director can step in as a third.

How it fits together

The browser draws, a Python orchestrator runs the conversation and the performance, and each AI model runs as its own local service.

The systems

Each was built as its own phase, with its own test.

Conversation and personality

  • Listening. Silero voice detection finds where speech starts and ends, and merges a pause of under 2 s into one turn.
  • Thinking. A local Qwen 27B streams the reply, preloaded so the first word arrives in about a second.
  • Two prompt layers. A performer contract sets how to act; the character's own sheet sets who it is, including its limits.
  • Persona pyramid. A 350-token core always in mind, eight summarised sections, and memories in its own words, recalled by what was just said.

Voice

  • Hearing. faster-whisper transcribes 1.4 s of speech in 245 ms.
  • Two engines. Chatterbox Turbo runs faster than real time, so there are no gaps between sentences. IndexTTS-2.5 can put anger or sadness in the voice.
  • Streaming. Each sentence is voiced while the model is still writing the next.
  • Talking over it. Interrupt and its voice ducks; it carries on if you gave a note, and stops if you started a conversation.

Face, lips and emotion

  • Lip sync. Every word is aligned, looked up in a 126k-word pronouncing dictionary and mapped to 18 visemes, so the lips really close on p, b and m.
  • Life layer. Blinks, micro-saccades, breathing and eyes that settle on the viewer, with the head following part of the way.
  • 37 acting emotions. Each one changes the face, the head pose and the voice together.
  • One performance. A director's note outranks the character's mood, which outranks its baseline.

Direction

  • Spoken notes. Mixed into normal talk, no mode button and no extra model call: expressions with intensity, gaze, posture, hold, relax.
  • No false triggers. 45 command phrasings recognised, 0 of 25 ordinary sentences ("Stop the car", "I can't stand it") mistaken for one.
  • Retakes. "Again" re-voices the last line with the new note. Every cue, line and take is logged.

The photoreal head

  • One photo, 20,018 Gaussians. LAM builds a splat head from a single photo, rigged onto the animated FLAME face so it moves with every expression, the jaw and the eyes.
  • A custom splat renderer. Sorted every frame in about half a millisecond, with teeth that show only through the lips.
  • Hair and ears. A second pass on a bald edit of the same photo gives real ears and scalp, and spring weights let the hair swing.
  • Accessories. Glasses or any 3D piece locked to the head.

Body and scenes

  • Bodies. MakeHuman figures on a Mixamo skeleton, with 264 shape sliders, clothes and painted skin.
  • Two actors. Each with its own head, body, life layer, voice and lip sync, looking at each other as they speak.
  • The director. Direct either actor by name, add notes mid-scene, call cut, or join as a third character.

How it was made

I designed Method, chose every approach, supplied the photos, voices and motion clips, and tested every piece on my own PC and headset. When a result didn't meet my bar for realism, the approach changed: a fitted, painted face that read as a video game gave way to the Gaussian head, and loudness-based lip sync gave way to matching every sound.

The code was written with Claude, an AI from Anthropic, as my pair programmer. It wrote and tested the code in a cloud workspace with no GPU, so everything that needed the real models was proven on my machine.

  1. A living checklist. One phased build checklist, carried from session to session, holding every decision and measurement.
  2. Whole files, versioned. Every delivery was complete files with a version bumped in each one, checked first on screen before anything else was tested.
  3. One task, one test. Nothing counted as done until I had run its test steps.
  4. Measured claims. Millimetres of error and milliseconds per frame, not "it works".
  5. Hard limits. Windows only, everything local, no paid services. Those limits are why the face fitter and the hair system are custom.
